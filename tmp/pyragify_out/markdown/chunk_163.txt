{'header': '### Role in the Campaign', 'content': '\nIn almost any settlement, from city to hamlet, you will find someone skilled in working metal. The art of turning iron and steel into the necessities of life – everything from nails to horseshoes to axeheads – is an essential part of any town. The local blacksmith is usually a respected craftsman, honored for his service to the community.\n\nFar more rare is the artiste whose skill with metal is such that he specializes in the crafting of armor. It is true that almost any metalsmith could turn out a functional shield, or perhaps a simple helm, but the crafting of suits of intricately linked mail or finely jointed plate armor requires a master’s touch. In a dangerous world full of creatures and villains eager to take your gold or your life, don’t you owe it to yourself to don the very best armor that money can buy?\n\nOutside of most major cities, actual armorers are likely to be rare. After all, few honest folk have much need for significant quantities of heavy weapons. At best they will rely on the village blacksmith to turn out the occasional spear point or round helm for use in the village militia.\n\nTrue masters of the art of armor crafting will most often be found in the employ of nobles or other representatives of the government of the day. In many jurisdictions, the making of armor will be controlled by the authorities so as to prevent any malcontents from arming themselves. However, large population centers – particularly those with strong merchants guilds, will usually offer enough of a market for one or more armorers to establish themselves. The same might also be true of smaller population centers in wild areas, prone to incursions by enemies or monsters. Any area heavily frequented by adventurers is also likely to attract craftsmen capable of fulfilling their particular\n\nneeds.\n\nOne way of introducing an additional air of mystique to a campaign is to introduce one or more craftsmen of such exceptional skill that their work is sought after not only for the quality of the items produced, but even for the prestige which accompanies the ownership of such an item. This is a simple way for a DM to introduce interesting and unique items into the game without necessarily always trotting out the same old magic items. If a DM works at building the aura of awe around such items as a master work suit of armor by a legendary armorsmith will be valued, even they don’t immediately convey tremendous defensive bonuses. Such items should be kept rare and expensive in order to increase their allure. After all, convincing a player that a suit of armor crafted by the incomparable Coralon is a worthy treasure is only going to be possible if the character in question isn’t constantly bumping into other people wearing similar items.\n\nIn addition to the possibilities presented by creating individual craftsmen of great repute, many games take as a given that a particular race or nationality has an innate ability for working metal. Whether it be dwarven armor or Damascene steel, there is a lot of room for DMs to create unique items of special significance.\n\n'}

{'header': '## Variations on the theme', 'content': '\nIn reality, many complicated suits of armor have to be custom made to fit their owner. Consider making it necessary for adventurers to seek out a master craftsman to have their new-found magical plate armor altered. Make this particularly nerve- wracking by insisting that characters leave the armor with the smith for several weeks. Perhaps the master is particularly old, crusty, and absent-minded…\n\nIt can add color to a campaign to designate certain exotic types of armor as being only made/worn by members of a particular race, class, social caste, guild, or faith. Choosing armor types from a radically different historical period or culture can make this a particularly powerful plot device. A race of mysterious goblinoids wearing an unknown style of laminated wooden armor is more interesting than yet another goblin in leather togs.\n\nOther possible reasons for visiting an armorer include maintenance and repair, commissioning of particularly extravagant items (solid gold breastplates etc), or appraising or selling loot acquired from the latest dungeon crawl. Especially creative players may want to design and have made a brand new style of armor, and will need to find a craftsman to transform their brilliant ideas into cold steel.\n\n'}

{'header': '## Overview', 'content': '\nCoralon the Armorer is known throughout the Kingdom and beyond as the finest craftsman working today. Certainly there are those who will claim that finer work is done deep in the Dwarven mountains or even in the shadows of the Elven woods, but none of those smiths have a shop right here in the city!\n\nCoralon has become quite wealthy as a result of his work and his shop is located in one of the finest merchant districts in the city. As his fame spread, he decided to relocate from his home village here to this teeming metropolis, as only the wealthiest patrons can now afford his work. “What is your life worth?” he will ask any who balk at paying the going rate for his work.\n\nThe shop is large and spacious, with a small showroom out in front. Although the showroom has a multitude of items on display, these are intended as examples, to help clients decide what they want made. Coralon works almost entirely on direct commission. He crafts individual pieces for each client. The items in the showroom are in fact of excellent quality but he is reluctant to sell them as this will necessitate making replacements. He can be talked into parting with basic items – shields, helms, gauntlets – by anyone willing to pay vastly inflated prices, but convincing him to part with one of the display suits of armor would require an astronomically large sum of money. Coralon works exclusively in metal. He considers the creation of “lesser” armor to be beneath his talents.\n\nCoralon’s shop is run by the master himself, and his much younger and prettier wife Deliah, who runs the showroom. He has three apprentices at various stages of development who help in the workshop.\n\nAlthough his shop holds very valuable merchandise, Coralon employs no visible\n\nsecurity. Even at night, the only visible sign of protection is the closed and locked doors and shutters. This is possible because of the invisible protection Coralon enjoys – a close and personal relationship with both the legal authorities and the powers that rule the underworld. Between the two, these groups see that Coralon’s riches tempt no one. He considers the occasional gifts of some of his better work to be a small price to pay to cultivate these relations.\n\n'}

{'header': '## Commissioning Armor', 'content': '\nAs noted above, Coralon does not sell “off the shelf”. Anyone seeking to engage the armorer to create a suit or piece of armor will need to come equipped with both patience and money. Coralon is constantly busy and there is generally a waiting list for his work. When accepting a commission he will warn the purchaser that it will be 1d6 months before he can even start on the project due to previous commitments. Coralon takes these commitments very seriously and rumor has it he has even made kings wait their turn, though few believe that to be true.\n\nEven once the work begins, it will take at least twice as long for Coralon to craft the work as it would normally. This is because of the painstaking attention to detail that he pours into each piece. He will not work faster, considering any efforts to goad him into do so as an insult to his art. Those who pester him excessively have been known to grow old waiting for their piece to be finished.\n\nEverything that Coralon produces should be considered masterwork.
